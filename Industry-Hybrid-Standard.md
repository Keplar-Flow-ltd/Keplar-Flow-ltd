**2. Related work**

_2.1. Industry 4.0 review (human-out-of-the-loop)_

Industry 4.0 is associated with the digital transformation of industrial (manufacturing, production, value creation,
etc.) processes piloted by German industry. The transformation was focused on smart factories benefiting from cyber-
physical systems, the Internet of Things, cloud computing, AI, machine learning and cognitive computing. The
“Industry 4.0” concept [14] has been introduced to promote the idea of machine (and, therefore, process) autonomy.
In [15], Industry 4.0 is considered as a new vision for a human-free manufacturing environment composed of product,
intelligence, machine-to-machine communication and networking.
An excellent review on Industry 4.0 has been provided in [16]. It argues that the Industry 4.0 paradigm assumes
that: robots will be more dominant in manufacturing; autonomous systems will make more self-decisions; processes
will be coordinated and problems will be solved without human involvement; and most of the communication will
take part between machines rather than between humans. Such smart manufacturing should improve the effectiveness
of data collection and analysis, make systems and processes more consistent, robust and agile, and, therefore, bring
more efficient business models. For Oztemel & Gursev [16], it is obvious that future manufacturing (according to the
Industry 4.0 philosophy) will be more smart, flexible, adaptive, autonomous, unmanned, and sensor based.
However, the need for human-centric approaches has been recognized already within the Industry 4.0 context.
Longo et al. [17] proposed an Industrial Internet pyramid (by combining technologies related to service-oriented digital
twins, ontologies, augmented reality, etc.) as an emergent human-centric manufacturing paradigm within Industry 4.
with the manufacturing employees playing the central role. Before that, Longo et al. [18] noticed that, in the very
dynamic working environment of Industry 4.0, human operators are required to be highly flexible and to demonstrate
adaptive capabilities. The study resulted in a framework for augmented operators, which relies on intelligent personal
digital assistants. Field experiments have proven that the approach is a human-centric one as it demonstrated a real
impact on the operators’ learning. The framework has been further developed by promoting the concept of “smart
operators” as enablers of human-centricity [19].

_2.2. Industry 5.0 review (human-back-into-the-loop)_

After about ten years since the introduction of Industry 4.0, the European Commission announced Industry 5.0 as
a response to the emerging societal challenges [12]. Industry 5.0 appeared as a vision of industry that aims beyond
efficiency and productivity towards respect to human values and contributing to vital society needs. It puts the workers’
wellbeing together with other human values (regarding the employees, the customers and society in general) at the
center of the manufacturing/production processes [20]. It also respects the production limits regarding the ecology of
our planet. It is considered as a transition to a human-centric [11], sustainable [21], and resilient [13] industry. Javaid
& Haleem [22] believe that modern industry needs the transition from the efficient use of industrial automation
(Industry 4.0) towards creating a new value from critical rethinking of human resources (Industry 5.0). According to
Maddikunta et al. [23], Industry 5.0 requires both an update of the enabling technologies (e.g., edge computing, digital
twins, collaborative robots, Internet of everything, etc.) and creation of new applications (e.g., in intelligent healthcare,
cloud manufacturing, supply chain management, etc.). Javaid et al. [24] describe technologies and applications that
fit the Industry 5.0 concept and are designed specifically to address the COVID-19 challenge.
The current state of Industry 5.0 regarding the related research trends has been analyzed in [25]. It has been
observed that the trends related to AI, big data, supply chain, digital transformation, machine learning, the internet of
things, are still among the key drivers for Industry 5.0 as they were for Industry 4.0.
Both Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0 rely on the extensive use of AI. However, the computer scientists working under
the Industry 5.0 framework usually talk about “weak AI”, which is used for specific tasks and is understandable and
manageable by humans. Therefore, the “human-in-the-loop” concept is seen as a strength, as it fosters transparent
man-machine cooperation, survives degraded modes in case of equipment failure and enables ethical decisions. As has
been noticed by Vogt [26], those who are working under the umbrella of Industry 4.0 support the “Black Box AI”,
which offers almost no human control.

The needs for humanization of the Industry 4.0 evolution and corresponding Industry 4.0 to Industry 5.

What are the views beyond Industry 5.0? Kuosmanen et al. [ 30 ] address the potential pathways the Finnish industry
must take in order to get leadership in future Industry 6.0. They believe that the recent global economic situation,
which is affected by many new crises and risks, requires a completely new industrial revolution, which will be focused
on “antifragility” to increase our resilience to future stressors and global shocks. Kuosmanen et al. ([ 30 ], p.7) define
Industry 6.0 as the “... ubiquitous, customer-driven, virtualized, antifragile manufacturing”, which enables customer-
centric hyper-connected multi-domain factories, with dynamic supply chains, where human (as a production worker)
becomes a part of the interconnected, digitalized, and optimized environment. Duggal et al. [ 31 ] believe that future
Industry 6.0 will be associated with the qualitative leap foreseen in further development of AI in general and robotics
in particular, which will be widely adopted and integrated into our daily lifestyle. As an example of such “another AI”
for Industry 6.0, Duggal et al. [ 31 ] expects AI to be trained to establish structural constraints based on the user’s
physical profile. This could lead up to a smarter manufacturing system wherein the user does not even have to enter
the product specifications in order to get the product done. Such user profiles could be designed as digital twins of the
users, as is mentioned in [ 32 ], to make a difference to the Industry 6.0 concept.

“Industry 4.0” concept [14] has been introduced to promote the idea of machine (and, therefore, process) autonomy.
In [15], Industry 4.0 is considered as a new vision for a human-free manufacturing environment composed of product,
intelligence, machine-to-machine communication and networking.
An excellent review on Industry 4.0 has been provided in [16]. It argues that the Industry 4.0 paradigm assumes
that: robots will be more dominant in manufacturing; autonomous systems will make more self-decisions; processes
will be coordinated and problems will be solved without human involvement; and most of the communication will
take part between machines rather than between humans. Such smart manufacturing should improve the effectiveness
of data collection and analysis, make systems and processes more consistent, robust and agile, and, therefore, bring
more efficient business models. For Oztemel & Gursev [16], it is obvious that future manufacturing (according to the
Industry 4.0 philosophy) will be more smart, flexible, adaptive, autonomous, unmanned, and sensor based.
However, the need for human-centric approaches has been recognized already within the Industry 4.0 context.
Longo et al. [17] proposed an Industrial Internet pyramid (by combining technologies related to service-oriented digital
twins, ontologies, augmented reality, etc.) as an emergent human-centric manufacturing paradigm within Industry 4.
with the manufacturing employees playing the central role. Before that, Longo et al. [18] noticed that, in the very
dynamic working environment of Industry 4.0, human operators are required to be highly flexible and to demonstrate
adaptive capabilities. The study resulted in a framework for augmented operators, which relies on intelligent personal
digital assistants. Field experiments have proven that the approach is a human-centric one as it demonstrated a real
impact on the operators’ learning. The framework has been further developed by promoting the concept of “smart
operators” as enablers of human-centricity [19].

_2.2. Industry 5.0 review (human-back-into-the-loop)_

After about ten years since the introduction of Industry 4.0, the European Commission announced Industry 5.0 as
a response to the emerging societal challenges [12]. Industry 5.0 appeared as a vision of industry that aims beyond
efficiency and productivity towards respect to human values and contributing to vital society needs. It puts the workers’
wellbeing together with other human values (regarding the employees, the customers and society in general) at the
center of the manufacturing/production processes [20]. It also respects the production limits regarding the ecology of
our planet. It is considered as a transition to a human-centric [11], sustainable [21], and resilient [13] industry. Javaid
& Haleem [22] believe that modern industry needs the transition from the efficient use of industrial automation
(Industry 4.0) towards creating a new value from critical rethinking of human resources (Industry 5.0). According to
Maddikunta et al. [23], Industry 5.0 requires both an update of the enabling technologies (e.g., edge computing, digital
twins, collaborative robots, Internet of everything, etc.) and creation of new applications (e.g., in intelligent healthcare,
cloud manufacturing, supply chain management, etc.). Javaid et al. [24] describe technologies and applications that
fit the Industry 5.0 concept and are designed specifically to address the COVID-19 challenge.
The current state of Industry 5.0 regarding the related research trends has been analyzed in [25]. It has been
observed that the trends related to AI, big data, supply chain, digital transformation, machine learning, the internet of
things, are still among the key drivers for Industry 5.0 as they were for Industry 4.0.
Both Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0 rely on the extensive use of AI. However, the computer scientists working under
the Industry 5.0 framework usually talk about “weak AI”, which is used for specific tasks and is understandable and
manageable by humans. Therefore, the “human-in-the-loop” concept is seen as a strength, as it fosters transparent
man-machine cooperation, survives degraded modes in case of equipment failure and enables ethical decisions. As has
been noticed by Vogt [26], those who are working under the umbrella of Industry 4.0 support the “Black Box AI”,
which offers almost no human control.

**5. The role of collective/collaborative intelligence**

Modern industrial processes contain many decision-making points, and the success of these processes depends on
the quality and efficiency of the decisions being made. If the progress of Industry 4.0 processes went towards
automated and autonomous decision-making, the processes of emerging Industry 5.0 are supposed to be human-in-
the-loop decision-making. Many decisions involve groups of decision-makers, which make corresponding processes
driven by collective intelligence. We may assume that, for the potential Industry 4.0 - Industry 5.0 hybrid, the collective
intelligence (as a driver of decision-making processes) will also be hybrid, i.e., will include humans and autonomous
automated decision-making components (agents). In this section, we will discuss the current status of collective
intelligence research and its readiness to support business processes of the future smart and hybrid manufacturing.
Collective intelligence enables a new “social” dimension (or layer) within the cyber-physical systems, making them
cyber-physical-social systems, i.e., self-organizational and crowd-powered ones [51]. This new dimension appears
also in the product manufacturing activities resulting in “social manufacturing” [52], which can be summarized as the
demand also for service principles, grouping and communities supported by complex collective intelligence networks.
Nguyen et al. [53], consider collective intelligence as a task-oriented intelligent activity of a group (collective) of
autonomous units (people or systems), and this activity has two important aspects: the cooperation (the units are
collaborative) aspect and the competition (the units are self-interested) aspect. The task is usually referred to making
common decisions, taking into account the aspects of integration, diversity and cardinality.
Williams [54] considers the so-called “general collective intelligence” as a necessary component to achieve
pervasive use of pervasive manufacturing. General collective intelligence is supposed to orchestrate groups to
cooperate as a single collective intelligence that greatly increases the group’s general problem-solving ability. Such
an increase in-group problem-solving ability, if applied to manufacturing, facilitates decentralized processes not
possible otherwise. In smart manufacturing, the need for efficient use of collective intelligence in general and
computational collective intelligence in particular is increasing dramatically. Lykourentzou et al. [55] consider three
categories of collective intelligence (collaborative, competitive, and hybrid) and classify corresponding systems into
passive and active ones. The first group of systems relies on specific features of each individual and is coordinated in
a bottom-up manner, while in the second group, the individual behavior does not pre-exist and the coordination goes
top-down, driven by the system (or process) requirements as a whole.
If Lykourentzou et al. [55] see “hybrid” collective intelligence as a group, which involves both collaborative and
competitive actors, the more popular view regarding the “hybrid” nowadays is related to the “collaborative
intelligence” where human and artificial actors are joining forces [56]. This view fits the needs well of the emerging
Industry 4.0 - Industry 5.0 hybrid because it enables us to explore the value of collaboration between not only
autonomous machines (Industry 4.0) or humans (Industry 5.0) but also within heterogeneous teams, where machines
assist humans in their smart activity and vice versa when appropriate. Zhang et al. [57] noticed that current networked
unmanned systems from Industry 4.0 still do not have sufficient human-level intelligence and proposed a vision of
human-centric networked unmanned systems (clusters) where distributed autonomous systems and humans are
connected via knowledge to achieve cognition. Ansari et al. [58] consider the possibility for humans and machines not
only to operate but also to learn together, which may transform today's smart factory into a self-learning factory. Such
factories will be driven by processes capable of dynamically switching between human workforces and collaborative

**6. Industry robustness, resilience and sustainability**

Resilience is required as one of the important enablers of Industry 5.0. Together with the related concepts of
recovery, robustness and sustainability, resilience has been considered as a key concept of global change, which is
ongoing in industry, business, our world and society as a whole [60]. The complexity of the resilience concept within
Industry 5.0 in comparison to Industry 4.0 is due to the fact that one has to take into account all possible interactions
in the layer of collective (collaborative) intelligence (machine-to-machine, machine-to-human, human-to-machine,
and human-to-human). The vulnerabilities of industrial processes based on infrastructure and automation (Industry
4.0) will also include vulnerabilities related to the human-in-the-loop (Industry 5.0); and this is an important concern
to be addressed by resilience in the new context, especially for decision-making processes [61].
Despite the increasing interest in resilience, there is still a lack of a conceptual model to explain how the resilience
of one system relates to the resilience of other co-occurring systems. Ungar [62] identifies principles that explain
patterns of resilience of future industrial systems-of-systems in the context of resilience of the interconnected
subsystems (biological, psychological, social, cultural, economic, legal, communication, ecological, etc.). These
common principles include: resilience of a system is a process which appears in adversity or adversarial context and
which enables trade-offs between subsystems; a resilient system is complex, open, dynamic, and it promotes
connectivity; a resilient system demonstrates experimentation and learning and it includes diversity, redundancy, and
participation. Resilience according to these principles can be considered as a sequence of systemic interactions through
which collaborating actors (humans, artificial and autonomous agents, enabling platforms or ecosystems) secure the
resources required for sustainability in stressed environments [62].
The concept of a cyber-physical system-of-systems enhanced towards the Industry 5.0 level recognizes the
increased importance of system resilience with the capability to handle safety and security related events [63].
Resilience (or the ability of a system to withstand disturbances and keep its operation in a sustainable and safe mode)
requires special interactions between the humans, autonomous AI components and low-level automation. Linnosmaa
et al. [63] suggests such a resilience enabling framework, according to which the flow of decisions starts from the
human operators (enhanced by decision support components), then to AI-driven autonomous control components, and
finally to low level automation controls. Taking into account that disturbances can happen either internally (at any of
these three levels) or externally from the environment, a resilient system must be capable of mitigating the negative
effects and sustain its continuous operation.
Our studies and experiments on resilience ([42] and [46]) provided by the Pi-Mind digital cloning technologies and
related collective-intelligence-driven processes prove that digital cloning essentially improves the resilience of
industrial processes comparably to the ones solely driven by humans or automation.
As it is admitted in [64], for enabling resilient systems of the future, the challenge would be to design and develop
smooth human-machine teams for it. Here the concept of resilient Operator 5.0 has a great importance. As noticed in
[29], active agent-driven interfaces (for the IoT, services and humans) will be able to gather information about the
agenthood status of the new generation of operators (i.e., Operator 5.0), social machines, and social software systems,
and send a request for cooperation enabling a collaborative multi-agent environment (such as, e.g., [65] or [49]), in
which “twin agents” of actual humans and machines will facilitate communication between them and the cyber and
physical worlds.

**7. Conclusions**

This paper raises an old and timeless “robots vs. humans” issue (particularly “efficiency and ubiquity vs. humanism
and responsibility”) in the new “Industry 4.0 vs. Industry 5.0” context. It looks like the modern industry must make
the choice of further development towards one of these options, which seem to be very different, almost contradictory.

On the one hand, current global challenges and crises require human-in-the-loop (supported by the Industry 5.
concept) for responsible decision-making in new, challenging, complex and critical situations related to the secure
control of smart manufacturing processes. On the other hand, the same challenges require efficient (fast and
sustainable) control driven by ubiquitous autonomous automation (supported by the Industry 4.0 concept).
Therefore, in this paper, we raise a question of: whether it is possible to create a sustainable hybrid integrating both
concepts into one? Our answer is yes. In this paper, we argue that some recently appeared digital artifacts, such as,
cognitive clones, are carriers of the Industry 4.0 + Industry 5.0 hybrid ideology and technologies, such as, cognitive
cloning based on the Pi-Mind adversarial learning ([ 40 ], [ 41 ] and [ 42 ]) are its enablers. A digital cognitive clone of a
human decision-maker is an industrial compromise in the “robots vs. humans” dilemma, serving as a bridging concept
to marry automation-centric Industry 4.0 and human-centric Industry 5.0. Cognitive clones, as smart bridging
technological artifacts, keep particular humans (donors for the clones) within the loop of responsible decision-making
processes and, at the same time, make such human involvement ubiquitous and, therefore, efficient. In this paper, we
also argue that the collective intelligence concept (humans and their autonomous digital clones), as a collaborative
decision-making power, is capable of serving as an enabler for the collective-intelligence-centric and resilient Industry
4.0 - Industry 5.0 hybrid.
Therefore, as a summary, we claim that: while the Industry 4.0 concept assumes a high share of automation in the
decision-making and the Industry 5.0 concept assumes the leading role of humans in the decision-making, the Industry
4.0 + Industry 5.0 hybrid assumes collective (hybrid) intelligence to be the decision-making driver (efficient,
ubiquitous, human-centric, responsible and resilient).
Our future work will focus on further development of the training techniques regarding collective (collaborative)
intelligence as a driver for the Industry 4.0 + Industry 5.0 hybrid. Such a training must be arranged as a smart
combination of human and machine learning and it will include an adversarial dimension to guarantee resilience as a
learning outcome.
